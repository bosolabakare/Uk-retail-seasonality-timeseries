### Introduction

In this report I analyse monthly UK retail sales via mail order houses. My aim is to examine whether the series shows seasonality and to explain why this matters for forecasting and decision-making. Retail sales through mail order and online platforms are expected to show seasonal behaviour because consumer spending increases and decreases at certain times of the year for example, it increases during the Christmas period.

### Understanding Seasonality

Seasonality refers to patterns that recur within a specific period of time. In monthly data, annual seasonality repeats every 12 months (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021). The seasonal effect is still visible here because the data is not seasonally adjusted (NSA). If strong annual seasonality is present, we would expect to see significant autocorrelation at lag 12 in the autocorrelation function (ACF).

## Concept 1: Autocorrelation

Autocorrelation measures how much a time series is related to its own past values (Box et al., 2015). At lag 1, it checks whether this month's value predicts next month's. At lag 12, it checks whether this month predicts the same month next year. A value close to +1 means a strong link; a value close to 0 means no link. A simple way to think about it: if a shop is busy this month, is it also likely to be busy next month?

### Code: Autocorrelation

```python
# Autocorrelation
max_lag = 60
autocorr_values = []

for lag in range(1, max_lag + 1):
    corr = np.corrcoef(values[:-lag], values[lag:])[0, 1]
    autocorr_values.append(corr)

plt.figure(figsize=(10, 5))
plt.bar(range(1, max_lag + 1), autocorr_values, color='teal')
plt.title('Autocorrelation of Retail Sales, Lags 1–60 Months')
plt.xlabel('Lag (Months)')
plt.ylabel('Correlation')
plt.axhline(0, color='black', linewidth=0.8)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('figure2_acf.png', dpi=150)
plt.show()
```

**Figure 2:** Autocorrelation Function (ACF), lags 1–60 months.


Most bars sit above 0.9. Lag 1 = 0.98, meaning this month strongly predicts the next month. Lag 12 = 0.97, confirming the seasonal Christmas pattern repeats almost every year. Lag 24 = 0.93, showing the same pattern is still present two years back. This is a continuous, recurring pattern rather than a one-off coincidence. For a decision maker, this means past data is highly reliable for short-term forecasting (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021) — but it also means naive forecasts that ignore the 12-month cycle (e.g. simply extrapolating last month's value) will systematically miss seasonal peaks and troughs.

## Concept 2: Exponential Smoothing

Exponential smoothing is a forecasting method that reduces random fluctuation in a time series by giving more weight to recent observations, making it easier to produce stable, forward-looking predictions. The formula is:

**New forecast = α × (actual this month) + (1 − α) × (previous forecast)**

Alpha (α = 0.3) controls how reactive the model is: a small α reacts slowly and smooths heavily, while a large α reacts quickly to recent changes. With α = 0.3, 30% of the weight goes to the latest observed value and 70% carries forward from the running smoothed history (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021).

### Code: Simple Exponential Smoothing

```python
# Exponential smoothing
alpha = 0.3
ses = np.zeros(len(values))
ses[0] = values[0]

for i in range(1, len(values)):
    ses[i] = alpha * values[i] + (1 - alpha) * ses[i - 1]

# Root Mean Squared Error between the smoothed series and the actual values
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((values - ses) ** 2))
print(f'RMSE = {rmse:.1f}')

plt.figure(figsize=(11, 5))
plt.plot(month, values, label='Original Data', color='gray', alpha=0.6)
plt.plot(month, ses, label=f'Exponential Smoothing (alpha = {alpha})', color='darkorange')
plt.title('Simple Exponential Smoothing of Retail Sales')
plt.xlabel('Month')
plt.ylabel('Retail Sales Index')
plt.xticks(month[::24], rotation=45)
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('figure3_ses.png', dpi=150)
plt.show()
```

**Figure 3:** SES (α = 0.3) vs actual monthly sales. RMSE = 8.0 index points


The orange line in Figure 3 follows the overall trend but does not jump with every seasonal spike, smoothing out noise and making the underlying direction clearer. The smoothed series confirms that, although there is strong monthly variation, the upward trend and repeating seasonal pattern remain clear underneath it. The RMSE reported by the code above shows, on average, how many index points the smoothed forecast deviates from the actual value. During the COVID-19 pandemic the model lagged noticeably, because α = 0.3 is a cautious setting that responds slowly to sudden shocks. A higher α would react faster in a crisis, at the cost of being less stable during quieter periods (Gardner, 2006).
